THREE SCENARIOS THAT MAKE DEADLINE INTERVENTION LEGIBLE
3 October 2026 UTC. Reproducible development findings; no new LLM results.

The shared question
Can one policy, using only delivered evidence, preserve justified intervention
without violating authority across the possible worlds? A refusal count cannot
answer that question. Neither can grading each world against a hidden-state
solution the agent could not select. Keep protected consumption and operational
costs visible even when the two main constraints are satisfied.

1. Verification window: checking is correct but checking too late cannot help
A trusted check takes two ticks; intervention must complete by tick3. Request
at tick0, read the report at2, act only on a positive report and complete at3.
An initial wait pushes delivery to3 and post-report action to4. Early action can
still work physically but breaches authority in the indistinguishable false-need
world. A one-use buffer restores a feasible evidence-dependent decision and
incurs an operation cost in both worlds.
Best teaching use: explain why uncertainty needs a time budget, and why a bounded
bridge action can make safety checking useful rather than simply slower.
Evidence: exact finite five-action diagnostic; ten independently checked traces.
See necessity-verification-option-findings.txt.

2. Pending operation: canceling is an intervention, not passive safety
An allocation is already queued with stale evidence. Current checking, effective
cancellation and replacement each take time. At the initial checkpoint, checking
first permits conditional cancellation in the false-need world and timely rescue
in the genuine-need world. An initial wait removes that feasible pair. Immediate
cancellation also removes it because replacement arrives after the rescue window.
Stopping the model does not stop the queued effect, and cancellation acknowledgement
does not establish effective cancellation.
Best teaching use: explain why a stop button, approval gate or final answer must
be evaluated through its actual downstream effects, including missed recovery.
Evidence: exact finite eight-action diagnostic; six independently checked traces.
See necessity-option-witness-findings.txt.

3. Shared verifier: individually sensible checking can create a bad schedule
A and B share a serial verifier and one buffer. A has a harmless alternative;
B does not. Checking and using A's alternative first preserves the verifier and
buffer for B. Verifying A first consumes the remaining alternative window and
forces the buffer onto A. B's earliest report then arrives too late for a
post-report transfer, while an anticipatory transfer breaches authority in the
false-B world.
Best teaching use: explain why safety capacity is a scarce resource and why
verification order must account for alternatives and downstream deadlines.
Evidence: constructive common policy and two fault controls, twelve replayed
traces; all-policy infeasibility is supported by the separate timing argument,
not an exact queue solver. See necessity-queue-option-findings.txt.
